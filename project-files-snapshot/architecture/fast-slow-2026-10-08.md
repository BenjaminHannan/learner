# Fast and slow thinking: do we need both? (Thu Oct 8, ~8:15 PM ET)

Asked by Ben, 8:10 PM ET 10-08: "Humans have fast and slow systems. Do we need that or is our model good enough"
Labels: **shown** = read in our code or results, **suggested** = reasoned or from papers, **untested** = never run here. Nothing was run.
Companion note: `running-summary-2026-10-08.md` (same evening). B2 only; the village model is not discussed.

## 1. Short answer

- We do not need a second, separate system (suggested). One looped thinker gives both: few rounds = fast, many rounds = slow.
- What is missing is the model choosing how long to think, and thinking longer actually helping. H1 (learned stop) is the piece that adds
  that. It is built, never run, held until G1 says go, and already in the big-run recipe. So: not good enough yet, no recipe change.

## 2. What the model has today (shown, branch `claude/custom-reader-talker-4x309r`)

| Human idea | Our part | Status |
|---|---|---|
| Fast, automatic | Reader (Gemma + letter window) in one pass; answers with 0 rounds | 0 rounds scores 0 to 18% in_dist across arms and seeds (`RESULTS-EG2.md`), so the fast path is small |
| Slow, step by step | Looped thinker, 8 rounds, one calculator step per round | Hard questions need it: chain-5 is 99.5-99.7 at 8 rounds, 2.2-3.0 at 1 round (`RESULTS-EG2.md`, Test LR rows) |
| Paper and pencil | Exact calculator, now an outside tool (T1SDR) | Calculator off: program questions 0.6% (deep dive) |
| Deciding how hard to think | None: every question gets exactly 8 rounds | H1 adds it (below) |
| Thinking longer helps | Not yet: 16 rounds scored 1.6 to 1.9 worse than 8 (plain B2, q33 seeds) | H1 trains for it |

H1 (`custom_io/models/tool_h1.py`, PASS-MARKS addendum 21): a 257-number stop head after every round; the turn ends at the first round where
stop >= 0.5, at least 1 round, cap 32. It is trained on the model's own answers ("settled": right now, or no later round will be right), and
training draws 4, 8, 16 or 32 rounds per batch so extra rounds stop hurting. H1R (on T1SDR) is 3,314,389 numbers. Never run (shown).

## 3. What the papers say (suggested; abstracts and summaries)

- HRM (2506.21734): a 27M model with a fast low-level and a slow high-level module, about 40% on ARC-AGI-1. ARC Prize's re-test
  (arcprize.org/blog/hrm-analysis) found most of the gain came from the outer "draft, then halt or refine" loop and task augmentation, not the
  two-speed split. Later work says the gains come mostly from recurrence; one 2026 language-model study (2604.14442) argues the slow module
  does matter. Disputed.
- TRM (2510.04871): one tiny 2-layer network, looped, with no fast/slow split: 44.6% ARC-AGI-1 at 7M vs HRM's 40.3% at 27M.
- Taken together: a single looped network that can stop or continue is at least as good as two separate systems at small size. That is
  our design.
- Practice turns slow into fast in people (driving becomes automatic). The machine version is distilling step-by-step answers into direct
  ones ("Distilling System 2 into System 1", Yu et al. 2024, from memory, arXiv 2407.06023). Our sleep line is the nearest thing we have.

## 4. Would adding a separate fast system help our goals? (suggested, untested)

- Skills: no expected gain over H1; a second network adds parameters the thinker could use instead.
- Few-example learning: no direct link found.
- Scaling bar: a looped thinker can spend more rounds on hard questions without more parameters, which is the kind of advantage a plain
  model lacks. Whether it gains more per size step is what G1 tests (with fixed rounds) and H1 would extend.
- Rules: H1 fits them. The stop is learned and the deployed model decides its own rounds; the 0.5 threshold and the cap of 32 are one-time
  build choices.
- Side note: the zero-round "leak" mark (now reported, not gated, Ben 10:16 AM ET 10-08) is the fast path. With H1, easy questions
  answered after 1 round are fine as long as hard ones still use the thinker.
