What to test next, and why

Seven research helpers each read one corner of the literature on small thinking machines. Here is what they found, one clue we spotted in our own results, and the five tests most likely to move the finish line.

Where the model stands

300 / 300New mazes where the stop never fires and thinking runs to the 48-round limit.

What the seven angles found

  • Looped solversTRM and HRM win with two plain tricks: grade the answer after every round, and lots of free variety (each maze turned and flipped 8 ways). The fancy two-level design mattered little.
  • New kindsARC winners adapt at test time on turned and flipped copies. Removing those copies is their biggest single loss. Our ruler gives the net only the k mazes, never their copies.
  • SearchSmall nets learn search on puzzles like ours only with hundreds of thousands of fresh puzzles or a better way to grade answers when many are correct.
  • StoppingNets that settle on the same answer from different starting points can think longer safely. Nobody has measured whether ours settles.
  • SleepBig pretrained nets forget less. Plain nets slowly lose the ability to learn new things over many tasks, and scale only delays it.
  • MemoryFor assistant memory, which notes get found matters more than clever memory systems. Small readers fall apart on long chats.
  • Everything elseRepeating a layer is worth less than adding a new one when models grow, so "the loop's lead holds at bigger sizes" still needs a real test.

A clue in our own numbers

The stop usually runs to the limit even when the answer is already right. But after one long sleep it suddenly works: 8 of 300 hit the limit instead of 300. Sleep never trains the stop part. It only changes the rest of the net. That suggests the stop part is fine, and the thinking on mazes just doesn't "look familiar" to it yet.

Seed 0, 300 held-out 9×9 mazes. Bar = mazes that hit the 48-round limit. The number inside each bar is mazes answered right. Source: RESULTS-EQ.md holdout table.

The cheapest big lever: eight views of one maze

A maze turned or flipped is still a maze with the same answer. The best tiny solvers train on all eight views. Ours never sees them when it learns a new kind. Test 1 gives it the eight views and costs no extra training steps.

One 7×7 maze in its 8 symmetric views. The teal cells are the answer path, and it moves with the maze.

The five tests, ranked

Ranked by chance of passing × how much of the finish line it moves × whether it keeps helping with use and size. Chances are our guesses. Pass marks are fixed before any run.

  1. 1

    Show it all eight views of each new maze

    few examplescarry-over~35%CPU or GPU, $0
    Change
    While learning mazes from k examples, turn or flip each one at random. Same examples, same number of steps. Every net gets the same treatment.
    Pass
    Practised loop gains at least 8 points on the maze score (at least 4 in each seed), and still beats the plain net by as much as today.
    Proved wrong
    Gain under 4 in both seeds, or the plain net gains just as much.
    Why it scales
    Every example you give it counts eight times. That helps any size of model.
  2. 2

    Watch the saved nets think, round by round

    own thinking timeno trainingMac CPU, $0
    Change
    None. For each of the 48 rounds, record how much the thought moves, the stop signal, and whether the answer flips. Compare against a run from a different starting point, and against the net after sleep.
    Decides
    Whether the stop part, a flickering answer, or thinking that never settles is to blame. That tells us which of the three queued stop fixes to trust.
    Proved wrong
    All three signals look fine, yet the stop still rarely fires.
  3. 3

    Grade number puzzles against the nearest correct answer

    search~25%GPU ~2 h, $0
    Change
    Today the "right answer" is whichever one a brute-force search finds first. Copying that means redoing the search, dead ends and all. Instead, count the net right when it matches any correct answer, and teach it toward the closest one.
    Pass
    It fits practice (90% or more valid) and solves at least 36 of 300 new puzzles in both seeds.
    Proved wrong
    It still can't fit practice (under 60%).
  4. 4

    Five days, five new kinds: does it keep learning?

    sleepgets better with useGPU 2–4 h, $0
    Change
    Each night, practise old kinds on fresh generated puzzles instead of 16 stored ones. After every night, check how fast it learns 64 new mazes.
    Pass
    Old kinds kept clearly better than with the small store, and learning speed on night 5 is within 8 points of night 0.
    Proved wrong
    No gain over the store, or learning speed falls by more than 15 points. That would be the "slowly loses plasticity" problem.
  5. 5

    Can the talker read the right notes?

    notebook memorydecisive either wayGPU <1 h, $0
    Change
    On 100 practice questions from the memory benchmark, hand the 1.2B talker the perfect notes, the top 10 found notes, or those 10 rewritten as short facts. The other 400 questions stay sealed.
    Pass
    Perfect notes give 50% or more, and short facts beat raw notes by 10 points.
    Proved wrong
    Under 30% even with perfect notes. Then the talker is the bottleneck, not the search.
