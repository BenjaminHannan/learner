# Angle 3: search inside the model (latent starts, refinement, self-training on Countdown / 24 / Sudoku)

Written 2026-09-29T01:55Z by a research agent (Sonnet). Research only: nothing run, no repo file changed except this one.
Labels: **shown** (a measured result in a source or a repo file I cite) / **suggested** (my reasoning) / **untested**. "Author-only" = the paper's own numbers, not reproduced by anyone I found. "Abstract" = I read only the abstract page or an HTML summary, not the full method.
Small card experiments (numbers, mazes, sums) and the village model are kept apart; everything here is about the small reasoner.

## 1. What the numbers puzzle is (shown, from code)
- The 24-game as a fill-in task: 4 numbers from 1-13 plus target 24; the net must write a 7-token postfix expression (for example `3 8 * 6 4 - *`) that evaluates to 24 (`scripts/claude_rsn358a_envs.py:242-274`, `number_item`, `check_numbers`). 1,362 of the 1,820 possible hands are solvable; 300 are held out for test, 1,062 are practice (`envs.py:292-299`).
- The training target is ONE stored answer per hand (the solver's first find), but the grader accepts any valid answer. Median 22 valid answers per hand (DIAGNOSIS.md cause 3).
- The read head writes all answer cells in one parallel pass per round (`Net.read(h)`, `claude_rsn358a_run.py:126`, per-cell logits, cross-entropy per cell). There is no left-to-right commitment. **Suggested, important:** with many valid answers per hand, independent per-cell guesses mix incompatible answers. Shown symptom: the fitted net's answers on the 300 held-out hands are 103 not-valid-postfix, 110 valid postfix with wrong numbers, 85 right numbers wrong value, 2 right (DIAGNOSIS.md "fitted net"). That is what a per-cell mixture of several styles looks like, and it is also what plain lookup failure looks like; the two are not separated by any run.

## 2. Interpreting the H2 clue (bigger pool, loop could not fit)
Shown (RECOUNT.md): pool 36,782 (hand, target) pairs, 69 draws per pair. Plain net: exactness 1.0 on the pool, held-out numbers4 6 and 4 of 300, P_other 7 and 10 (floor 12). Loop: exactness 0.38 and 0.56, numbers4 4 and 4, P_other 7 and 10; loop still perfect on sums4/grids5.
Readings, ranked by how well they fit (all suggested / untested):
1. **The stored answer is an unlearnable tie-break.** Among ~8 to 22 valid answers the solver's choice is an arbitrary function of the pair. Fitting it needs either memory (plain net has 8 distinct blocks, so it stores 37k rows) or actually knowing the solver's search order. A weight-shared 2-block loop is a poor lookup table, so it cannot store 37k arbitrary rows, and it has not found a rule. So "cannot fit" says: **the loop has no cheap way to fit this target, not that the pool was too small.** This predicts that lowering the target's entropy (make the stored answer a learnable function, or accept any valid answer) should raise exactness on the SAME pool. That is a one-change test (T1 below).
2. **Memorising is the plain net's easy route, not the loop's.** The plain net went straight to 1.0 with held-out at the floor: at 69 draws per pair it still prefers lookup. Ye et al. (below) show that at ~450k fresh problems a 6M non-autoregressive net reaches 52% on Countdown-4, so pool size does matter, but in their case the objective was different (see source 1). Scale of distinct puzzles helps only if the objective lets a small net fit by rule.
3. Not supported: "more rounds fix it". Shown: 0 to 3 of 300 at every round count (DIAGNOSIS.md). The state settles to a per-hand fixed point.
So unlimited fresh puzzles alone is unlikely to be enough (suggested). The clue points to fixing the target/objective first, then the pool.

## 3. Sources (strongest first)

1. **Ye et al. 2024, "Beyond Autoregression: Discrete Diffusion for Complex Reasoning and Planning" (ICLR 2025)**, https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.14157, code https://github.com/HKUNLP/diffusion-vs-ar. Read: abstract page + HTML summary (setup numbers from the HTML page; method details not read in full). Shown (author-only): Countdown with 3/4/5 numbers, 500,000 generated problems, targets 10-100, 10% of targets held out for out-of-distribution; MGDM 6M params 52.0% on Countdown-4, 85M params 91.5% (Countdown-5: 46.6%), autoregressive 85M 45.8%. Sudoku: 100,000 games, 6M-param MGDM 100% vs LLaMA-13B 32.9%. Their explanation, "subgoal imbalance": some intermediate targets are much harder to learn than others; the diffusion loss (training across all masking levels, weighted toward hard positions) spends more effort on them. Maps to us: our "hard subgoal" is the choice of number order and operators, which needs arithmetic; our one-shot cross-entropy treats it like copying a skeleton. The dataset is 450k fresh problems, not 1,062 hands; their gain is confounded with data size and with model size (6M vs 85M is a large gap). Weak points: I did not verify that the AR baseline was given the same data and steps; treat "diffusion beats AR" as author-only.
2. **Gandhi et al. 2024, Stream of Search**, https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.03683 (already covered by G; I add only the numbers relevant to the pool question). Read: HTML page. Shown (author-only): 250M-param GPT-Neo, 500,000 Countdown problems, evaluated on held-out inputs (51.27% search-trace model vs 25.73% optimal-path-only model) and on 10% held-out targets. STaR/APA on the model then solved ~36% of previously-unsolved training problems and ~4% of 10,000 hard problems no symbolic strategy solves. The lesson for us that G does not use: even the best-cited small-model Countdown result used ~500k distinct problems and a model 40x our size; our 1,062 practice hands is 500x fewer. Also they train on search traces (kind-specific data; a listed fallback in G, not proposed here).
3. **TRM, Jolicoeur-Martineau 2025, "Less is More: Recursive Reasoning with Tiny Networks"**, https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.04871 (and HRM, Wang et al. 2025). Read: HTML summary via fetch tool. Shown (author-only, one group): Sudoku-Extreme 87.4% (HRM 55.0%), Maze-Hard 85.3%, 7M params, 2 layers, N_sup = 16 deep-supervision steps, and deep recursion/supervision "bypasses a lot of the overfitting"; adding layers hurt generalisation. Data: 1,000 training puzzles x 1,000 symmetry shuffles (Sudoku row/column/digit permutations) = 1M distinct instances. Our loop is the same design family (shared 2 blocks, deep supervision, halt on "is exactly right"). Two differences that matter: (a) their augmentation makes every step a new instance while keeping the label a deterministic function; (b) Sudoku has a UNIQUE solution, so there is no tie-break entropy. Numbers have neither property. I could not confirm from the page how TRM initialises its latent (the summary says only learnable init embeddings), so I make no claim that TRM uses stochastic starts.
4. **Geiping et al. 2025, "Scaling up Test-Time Compute with Latent Reasoning" (recurrent depth, Huginn)**, https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.05171. Read: abstract only. Shown (author-only, 3.5B params): a recurrent block unrolled to any depth at test time improves reasoning benchmarks. I recall from memory (not verified here) that its latent starts from a random Gaussian draw and training backprops through only the last few iterations, which is the same truncation as our GRAD_ROUNDS = 6; treat this as a lead. Relevance: the natural, weight-free source of "multiple starts" is noise, and it was done at scale. Not shown at our size, and not shown to give diverse solutions on a puzzle with many answers.
5. **Bansal et al. 2022, "End-to-end Algorithm Synthesis with Recurrent Networks: Extrapolation without Overthinking"** (Schwarzschild et al. 2021 for the maze/Sudoku forerunner), https://arxiv.org/abs/2202.05826 (memory, not opened this session). Recalled: input re-injection ("recall", we have it) plus training with a mix of iteration counts (we have it) is what makes a looped net extrapolate in rounds. It also reports that overthinking, where the state drifts after solving, is prevented by a stable fixed-point design. Consistent with our shown observation that extra rounds change the answer without improving it.
Also read as context, not as leads: Searchformer (Lehnert et al., https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.14083, abstract via fetch: solves unseen Sokoban 93.7%, with a 5-10x smaller model and 10x less data than solution-only training, author-only) and Dualformer (https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.09918, abstract: randomly dropping parts of the search trace gives fast, slow and auto modes; 97.6% optimal on 30x30 mazes, author-only). Both need search traces as targets, which is the "solver-written traces" fallback already noted in G, so I do not propose them. IRED (Du et al., https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.11179, abstract only): learns a sequence of energy landscapes and adapts the number of optimisation steps to difficulty; solves harder Sudoku and connectivity than training, author-only. It changes the whole architecture and objective, so it is not one change. Recurrent Relational Networks (Palm et al., https://arxiv.org/abs/1711.08028, abstract only: 96.6% on hardest Sudoku, loss applied at every step) is the ancestor of our per-round loss. SATNet: not opened; I recall a later paper questioning whether it solves the symbol-grounding part, so I do not lean on it.

## 4. Key question: why does the tiny net memorise, and what minimal change makes it learn search?
Short answer (suggested, untested): three things stack.
- **(a) Memorising is cheaper than the rule.** 1,062 hands x 2,410 repeats is a table that a 1.6M-weight net fits by step ~20k. Shown.
- **(b) The label carries no rule.** One arbitrary answer among ~22 valid ones. Even on fresh hands, cross-entropy on an arbitrary tie-break punishes the net for choosing a different valid answer. This is why the numbers task is not Sudoku (unique solution, TRM) and why any-valid or canonical targets are the natural fix. Not shown for this net.
- **(c) One parallel read per round has no way to commit to a joint answer** (section 1). Diffusion-style unmasking commits a few tokens per round and re-conditions; that is what Ye et al. does.
Ranking of the "minimal change" list from the request, by how well the evidence supports it for THIS puzzle:
1. **Lower the target's entropy (canonical answer, or accept any valid answer)**: best supported by the H2 clue and by the Sudoku-vs-numbers contrast. Cheapest.
2. **Unlimited fresh puzzles**: necessary but, alone, probably not sufficient (H2 clue, section 2). Note the fresh-pair pool can be made huge without touching the test: widen the target range (1820 hands x 150 targets = 273k pairs, 9 draws per pair over 2.56M draws; SoS/Ye used ~500k). Suggested.
3. **Noise in the carried latent / random start**: diversity source and regulariser; unproven for tie-break-heavy targets. G already covers "several starts, winner-take-all, pick by halt"; noise only replaces the 4 learned vectors with unlimited free draws. Minor difference, so I make it a follow-on, not a first test.
4. **Curriculum by search depth**: for four numbers the depth is fixed (3 operations); a curriculum would run 2-number, then 3-number, then 4-number hands. The 3-number stream already exists and also fails to transfer (numbers5 0 to 2 of 300, numbers3 already at 1,902 repeats per item). Not enough evidence to pick it. Untested.
5. **Diffusion-style masked denoising objective**: strongest external evidence (source 1) but the biggest change and confounded by scale in the paper. It is my T2.

## 5. Mapping to Premonition's open problems
- **Numbers 4/300 vs floor 12.** Needs (b) fixed first (T1), then a way to commit (T2), then G-style diversity.
- **Mazes.** Mazes have (near-)unique shortest paths or a fixed answer format; the memorisation problem does not appear (held-out kind). Shown effect of practice on mazes is carry-over from grids. Nothing here says numbers fixes will move F_eq; they may help only if a canonical/any-valid habit transfers. Untested. Any numbers change must be checked that it does not drop the shown maze ruler (F_eq 51.0) or sums/grids.
- **The stop.** Learned stop never fires on new mazes. A search-inside-the-model angle gives one cheap read-only idea (suggested, untested, no training): run K = 4 noisy starts on a new maze and stop when they agree; agreement across DIFFERENT starts is a cross-check that answer-stability across rounds (standing 02, SL) cannot give, because a settled state is stable even when wrong. Only worth trying after T3 exists; it would cost inference time only.
- **Improves with use / scales.** Fresh generated puzzles and self-sampled successes scale with use; a canonical target or masked objective does not depend on model size. Ye et al.'s gap between 6M (52%) and 85M (91.5%) is at least a hint (author-only) that this family gains from size, and Ben expects a much bigger real model.

## 6. Tests (one change each, marks fixed now)
Common setup: the H2/G comparator set. Same sealed 358u code, loop arm and plain-net row, seeds 13 and 14 (2 seeds; add 15, 16 only if a PASS is claimed), 60,000 steps, batch 256, same 300 held-out hands (target 24), kind-blind (env fixed 0), no checker at test time. Baselines (shown): loop 1 to 3 of 300 (358u/358u2) and 4, 4 (H2 pool); plain 4 to 6; floor F = 12. Cost per 4-net batch: about $0.5 and 70 min on a 5090 (DIAGNOSIS.md); estimate 1.5 to 2 h for 2 loop + 2 plain nets on a 5070 Ti 16 GB (suggested, not measured); Mac CPU is not practical for 60k steps of a 6.3M net. Also report sums4/grids5 (gate: at least 285 of 300 each, as H2 marks) and practice exactness. Where H2's own marks say noise band, 9 to 29 of 300 is "no claim".

### T1. Canonical target on the H2 pool (top pick)
- One change: keep the H2 pool of 36,782 (hand, target) pairs and everything else; replace the solver's first-found stored answer by a deterministic canonical valid answer chosen by a fixed rule from the enumerated valid answers (most frequent skeleton in the pool first, then numbers in ascending order). Code-made data, kind-blind. It is different from H2's any-valid fallback (which changes the grader and halt label but still trains on one stored answer) and from G (multiple starts).
- Why: tests reading 1 of section 2 directly. If the tie-break was the problem, the loop should now fit the SAME pool.
- Pass marks (fixed now): **fit** = loop practice exactness (grader validity, mean of last 3 log lines) at least 0.90 on both loop seeds, where H2 gave 0.38 and 0.56. **Generalise** = numbers4 at least 36 of 300 (3 x floor 12) on both loop seeds. Plain-net row reported; a PASS needs plain at most 12 (otherwise the gain is not specific to the loop).
- Proves the idea wrong: loop practice exactness still below 0.6 on both seeds (then entropy of the target is NOT why the loop cannot fit, and reading 1 is dead). If exactness is at least 0.90 but numbers4 at most 12 on both seeds: it now memorises the canonical answers; the fit clue was capacity, and the pool must grow (T1 becomes step one of a larger-pool test, not a failure of the idea). 13 to 35 is partial, no claim.
- Cost: 2 loop + 2 plain nets, about 2 h, $0.6 on rented GPU. Enumerator already exists (`claude_numbers_diag_answers.py`).

### T2. Masked-answer denoising (Ye-style), only after T1 reads
- One change: each training step, reveal a random fraction (uniform 0 to 1) of the target answer tokens at the answer cells as input (the rest stay masked) and train cross-entropy on the masked cells only; at test start with everything masked, and use the loop rounds to commit the most confident cell each round. Uses the T1 canonical target so the label is learnable. Difference from R3 "Refill" (mentioned in G's survey as another thread's; I did not find its design file): R3 feeds the model's OWN previous answer back; T2 trains on ground-truth partial reveals of random size, which is what teaches "is this token consistent with the others". Check with the R3 owner before running; if they overlap, use R3's result instead.
- Pass marks: numbers4 at least 36 of 300 on both loop seeds AND at least 12 more than the T1 loop result on both seeds (so T2 must add something beyond T1). Sums4/grids5 gate as above.
- Proves the idea wrong: numbers4 within 6 of the T1 result (or at most 12) on both seeds; or the model reaches its answers only when at least half the answer is revealed (report exactness at reveal fractions 0, 0.25, 0.5 on held-out hands to see whether it learned to check or only to copy).
- Cost: roughly the T1 cost plus a code change to the reader inputs; about 2 to 3 h on the 5070 Ti (suggested).

### T3. Gaussian noise in the initial and carried latent (cheap, optional)
- One change: `h = torch.randn_like(e) * sigma` instead of zeros in `loop_train`/`loop_rounds`, sigma fixed at 0.1 of the state's scale (single value, no tuning). No extra weights, no winner-take-all, no per-stream loss. Differs from G: G learns 4 start vectors and trains winner-take-all; T3 draws unlimited free starts and trains a single ordinary loss. Run on top of T1 (fit must already work).
- Read-only at test: K = 4 noisy starts, pick by halt head (S_pick) vs oracle (S_any) vs one start (S_rand), same split as G's PASSMARKS.
- Pass marks: numbers4 at least 12 more than T1's on both seeds (single start), and S_any minus S_rand at least 20 of 300 on both seeds (evidence that starts diverge); maze F_eq (practised loop 51.0/51.3) must not drop more than 4 (F_eq noise sd 3.3 to 4.2), sums4/grids5 gate.
- Proves the idea wrong: S_any within 6 of S_rand on both seeds (noise gave no diversity; the settled state ignores the start), or F_eq falls by more than 8.
- Cost: cheapest, about 1.5 h on the 5070 Ti; the maze F_eq run is the extra cost.

If T1 fails (loop still cannot fit), the sensible next step is not T2 but the fresh, much larger pool with the canonical target (273k pairs). That would be a second change and needs its own marks.
