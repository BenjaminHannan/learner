### [VERIFIED] C1 (part a). Yao et al. define Reliability, Generalization (rephrased prompts), and Locality (unrelated inputs unchanged).
  LOC: Section 2 'Problems Definition', paragraphs headed Reliability / Generalization / Locality (Eqs. 2-4)
  PASSAGE: define a reliable edit when the post-edit model gives the target answer for the case to be edited. [...] The post-edit model should also edit the equivalent neighbour (e.g. rephrased sentences). [...] Editing should be implemented locally, which means the post-edit model should not change the output of the irrelevant examples in the out-of-scope
  NOTE: Section 2 frames these as 'three properties: reliability, generalization, and locality'. Generalization is measured as average accuracy on examples drawn from the equivalence neighborhood N(x_e,y_e); Locality ('also noted as Specificity in some work') is the rate at which post-edit predictions are unchanged from the pre-edit model on out-of-scope O(x_e,y_e), which 'consists of inputs that are unrelated to the edit example'.

### [VERIFIED] C1 (part b). Yao et al. define Portability as edited knowledge transferring to related content, subdivided into subject aliasing (Subject Replace), Reversed Relation, and One-hop reasoning.
  LOC: Section 5.1 'Portability - Robust Generalization'
  PASSAGE: we introduce a new evaluation metric called Portability to gauge the effectiveness of model editing in transferring knowledge to related content [...] (1) Subject Replace [...] replacing the subject in the question with an alias or synonym. ### [VERIFIED] C4. MQuAKE's MeLLo stores edits in an explicit external memory and decomposes the question, i.e. it is retrieval, not weight editing.
  LOC: Section 1 Introduction (paragraph 5); also Section 2.1 and Section 5.1
  PASSAGE: Instead of updating model weights, MeLLo stores edits in an explicit memory inspired by memory-based editing methods Mitchell et al. (2022b) and prompts the language model iteratively to interact with the edited facts. Specifically, it decomposes a multi-hop question into sub-questions successively, generates tentative answers, and checks whether it is consistent with edited facts before returning the final answer.
  NOTE: Section 2.1: 'MeLLo, is much more lightweight: it keeps f frozen and instead uses E as an external knowledge store to guide generation.' Section 5.1: 'MeLLo keeps the base language model frozen and maintains all the edits in an explicit memory.' Section 5.1 details: edited facts are embedded with an off-the-shelf Contriever retriever into a retrieval index; each subquestion retrieves the most relevant edit statement, and the model self-checks for contradiction. MeLLo 'does not require any additional training'. Terminology caveat: the paper presents MeLLo as its proposed method, and suggests it 'can serve as a strong knowledge-editing baseline for future research', rather than calling it a baseline of the benchmark.

### [VERIFIED] C5 (part a). Cohen et al. define ripple-effect criteria Logical Generalization, Compositionality I, Compositionality II, Subject Aliasing, Preservation, Relation Specificity.
  LOC: Section 3 'Ripple Effects of Factual Edits', numbered criteria list (1)-(6), with examples in Fig. 2
  PASSAGE: Logical Generalization (LG): [...] check that such logical implications about the subject e, the original object o, and the new object o*, hold after editing. Compositionality I (CI): [...] compose the edited fact with other facts about the target object. Compositionality II (CII): [...] compose the edited fact with facts about a different subject e′≠e.
  NOTE: Remaining three verbatim: 'Subject Aliasing (SA): We test that editing a fact about e induces the same edit to other entities e′ that are aliases of e'; 'Preservation (PV): If r is a one-to-many relation, then adding a new object should not affect the other objects encoded about e'; 'Relation Specificity (RS): We test that facts about e, with relations whose objects are not influenced by o*, are indeed not affected by the edit.' LG examples: Sibling is symmetric, Location is transitive. CI expects (e, r∘r′, z) → (e, r∘r′, z*); CII expects (e′, r′∘r, o) → (e′, r′∘r, o*). Ripple effect is defined as 'the set of triplets that the model implicitly needs to inject, modify, or delete' after an edit; evaluation is limited to facts 'within a 2-hop distance from the subject or object of the edit'.

EXTRA:
- Yao et al. Section 5.2 'Locality - Side Effect of Model Editing' proposes three locality levels beyond standard benchmarks: (1) Other Relations ('other attributes of the subject that have been updated should remain unchanged'), (2) Distract Neighbourhood ('if we concatenate the edited cases before other unrelated input, the model tends to be swayed by the edited fact'), (3) Other Tasks (commonsense reasoning). Table 4 (GPT-J): methods 'excel in the other attributions aspect' but 'generally perform poorly in Distract-Neighbor settings' (FT-L 9.48, T-Patcher 17.56, MEND 32.96, SERAC 39.18, ROME 50.35, MEMIT 60.47, IKE 66.04); 'methods that alter parameters tend to negatively influence performance [on other tasks], with the exception of MEMIT.'
- Yao et al. Section 4.2 'Sequential Editing' and 'Batch Editing': the default protocol is to 'update a single model knowledge, evaluate the new model, and then roll back the update' per test point; under sequential (no-rollback) editing, 'Methods that freeze the model's parameters, like SERAC and T-Patcher, generally show stable performance ... those altering the model's parameters struggle. ROME performs well up to n=10, then degrades at n=100 ... MEND performs well at n=1 but significantly declines at n=10.' For batch editing, MEMIT's 'reliability and generalization remains robust up to 1000 edits, but locality decreases at this level'; MEND and FT-L decline rapidly with batch size (Figures 3-4).
- MQuAKE Section 4.2 interprets the failure as parameter fitting rather than knowledge integration: 'current knowledge-editing techniques, instead of integrating new facts into the model as new internal knowledge, are rather hard coding them into the model by updating weights locally.' Section 4.4 'Evaluation with Edits at Scale' (k ∈ {1,100,1000,3000} simultaneous instances on MQuAKE-cf): 'the multi-hop performance of all methods further drops when injecting more edits into language models at the same time' (Figure 2).
- MQuAKE Section 3.4 metric protocol: edit-wise success rate (each edited fact recalled via manually written cloze template), instance-wise accuracy (all facts in a chain recalled; measured pre- and post-edit), and multi-hop accuracy where each instance has three GPT-4-generated multi-hop question paraphrases and 'If any of the three questions is correctly answered by the model, we regard it as accurate.' Section 4.3: with chain-of-thought, 'explicit knowledge recall greatly helps the edited models answer multi-hop questions, while these models struggle to utilize the edited knowledge internally' (e.g., MEND on MQuAKE-t rises from 16.0 to 38.2 multi-hop with CoT, Table 4). Section 5.3 notes MeLLo's ceiling depends on the retriever retrieving 'all the associated edited facts' (Appendix I).
- Cohen et al. Section 5.1 evaluation protocol: data is filtered so that '(a) only cases of successful edits are evaluated, and (b) only test queries that the model answered correctly pre-editing are used'; for each criterion, accuracy is averaged over test queries per example then over examples; a generation of at most 20 tokens is correct 'if one of the aliases of the target object appears in the text'. RippleEdits has three subsets (Recent = real new facts to inject; Random and Popular = counterfactual edits differing in entity popularity) to control ripple-effect severity; 'Logical Generalization accuracy is substantially higher for Random, Preservation accuracy is higher for Popular' (Section 5.2).
- Cohen et al. on in-context vs. parametric editing and failure modes: the ICE baseline 'does not introduce changes to the model parameters, but rather generation is conditioned on the new fact' via the prompt 'Imagine that <o*> would have been <P_r>' (Section 5.1) and 'obtains the best overall results ... outperforms ROME by more than 10 points for GPT-Neo and 29 points for LLaMA' (Section 5.2); 'editing performance increases with model size' (Fig. 6), yet 'simply scaling the model size may not be sufficient'. Error analysis (Table 7): 'in most cases (≥68% across all settings) factual editing introduces erroneous changes to the model's knowledge rather than making no change.' Limitations: RippleEdits 'does not include tests, such as paraphrasing of the edit and subject specificity' and does not verify retention of distantly related facts.

### [VERIFIED] C1. GEM defines ACC (average final accuracy), BWT (backward transfer: average over tasks of final accuracy minus accuracy right after learning that task; negative values indicate forgetting) and FWT (forward transfer).
  LOC: Lopez-Paz & Ranzato, Section 2, subsection 'Training Protocol and Evaluation Metrics', Eqs. (2)-(4) (ar5iv full text)
  PASSAGE: we construct the matrix R∈ℝ^{T×T}, where R_{i,j} is the test classification accuracy of the model on task t_j after observing the last sample from task t_i. [...] ACC = (1/T) Σ_{i=1}^{T} R_{T,i} (2); BWT = (1/(T−1)) Σ_{i=1}^{T−1} R_{T,i} − R_{i,i} (3); FWT = (1/(T−1)) Σ_{i=2}^{T} R_{i−1,i} − b̄_i (4)
  NOTE: Equations transliterated from the paper's LaTeX (alttext) to Unicode; the LaTeX writes the BWT/FWT summands without parentheses, but the sum is over the difference (Appendix B reports scalar 'Backward'/'Forward' values consistent with this). b̄ is defined as 'the vector of test accuracies for each task at random initialization'. Same section: 'Large negative backward transfer is also known as (catastrophic) forgetting.' Note that BWT compares against R_{i,i}, accuracy right after finishing task i, not the max over later evaluations (see C3).

### [VERIFIED] C2. BWT in GEM is a SIGNED average (not clipped at zero).
  LOC: Lopez-Paz & Ranzato, Section 2 ('Training Protocol and Evaluation Metrics'), text around Eqs. (2)-(4); Table 3 (Sec. 4.4.1); Appendix B
  PASSAGE: Large negative backward transfer is also known as (catastrophic) forgetting. [...] The larger these metrics, the better the model. If two models have similar ACC, the most preferable one is the one with larger BWT and FWT.
  NOTE: Eq. (3) contains no max(0,·)/clipping. Reported values are signed on both sides of zero: Table 3 lists 'single 0.53/-0.08, 0.49/-0.25, 0.43/-0.40' (ACC/BWT) and Appendix B evaluation matrices report e.g. 'Backward: -0.2047' and 'Backward: 0.0042'. Sec. 3 states GEM uses memory losses 'as inequality constraints, avoiding their increase but allowing their decrease [...] our model therefore allows positive backward transfer', and Sec. 4.4 reports 'positive backward transfer in CIFAR100'. Chaudhry et al. independently confirm GEM's version is signed by noting their own f_j^k ∈ [−1,1] and that GEM uses a_{j,j} in place of the max.

### [VERIFIED] C3. Chaudhry et al. define a forgetting measure as the difference between the MAXIMUM accuracy previously achieved on a task and its current accuracy (so the measure uses a max over earlier evaluations).
  LOC: Chaudhry et al., Section 3 'Forgetting and Intransigence', subsection 'Forgetting Measure (F)', Eq. (3) (ar5iv full text)
  PASSAGE: We define forgetting for a particular task (or label) as the difference between the maximum knowledge gained about the task throughout the learning process in the past and the knowledge the model currently has about it. [...] f_j^k = max_{l∈{1,⋯,k−1}} a_{l,j} − a_{k,j}, ∀ j<k. (3)
  NOTE: a_{k,j} ∈ [0,1] is accuracy on the held-out test set of task j after incremental training through task k; f_j^k ∈ [−1,1]; average forgetting F_k = (1/(k−1)) Σ_{j=1}^{k−1} f_j^k, 'Lower F_k implies less forgetting'. The authors explicitly contrast with GEM ([15]): 'in [15], a_{j,j} is used in place of max_{l} a_{l,j} [...] which makes the measure agnostic to the il process and does not effectively capture forgetting', giving the example accuracies {0.7,0.8,0.6,0.5} where Eq. (3) yields 0.3 but GEM's version yields 0.2. Negative f_j^k is interpreted as positive backward transfer. Note the sign convention is opposite to GEM's BWT (higher F = worse).

### [VERIFIED] C4. van de Ven & Tolias define Task-IL, Domain-IL, Class-IL by whether task identity is provided at test time and whether it must be inferred.
  LOC: van de Ven & Tolias, Abstract; Section 2 'Three Continual Learning Scenarios', Table 1 and the three following paragraphs (ar5iv full text)
  PASSAGE: we describe three continual learning scenarios based on whether at test time task identity is provided and—in case it is not—whether it must be inferred. [...] Task-IL | Solve tasks so far, task-ID provided; Domain-IL | Solve tasks so far, task-ID not provided; Class-IL | Solve tasks so far and infer task-ID
  NOTE: Section 2 elaborates: Task-IL: 'models are always informed about which task needs to be performed. This is the easiest continual learning scenario'; Domain-IL: 'task identity is not available at test time. Models however only need to solve the task at hand; they are not required to infer which task it is'; Class-IL: 'models must be able to both solve each task seen so far and infer which task they are presented with'. The paper stresses the scenarios reflect evaluation conditions, not output-layer architecture (multi-head vs single-head).

### [PARTIAL] C5. GEM's protocol trains on each task's data once (single pass) with a small episodic memory of past examples.
  LOC: Lopez-Paz & Ranzato, Section 2 ('Training Protocol and Evaluation Metrics') and Section 3 (GEM); also Sec. 4.1: 'The model observes the tasks in sequence, and each example once.'; Sec. 4.2; Appendix A
  PASSAGE: at training time we provide the learner with only one example at the time (or a small mini-batch) [...] The learner never experiences the same example twice, and tasks are streamed in sequence. [...] the learner has a total budget of M memory locations. If the number of total tasks T is known, we can allocate m=M/T memories for each task.
  NOTE: Single pass is verified (Sec. 2 lists 'the learner observes the examples concerning each task only once' as a design goal; Sec. 4.2 uses 'plain SGD on mini-batches of 10 samples'). The caveat is 'small': Appendix A gives GEM's memory size as '5120 (rot, perm, cifar)', i.e. 256 examples per task with T=20. With 1000 examples/task on the MNIST variants (20,000 total) that is ~25.6% of the training stream; on CIFAR-100 (2500/task, 50,000 total) ~10%. Memory is populated with 'the last m examples from each task'. Table 2 shows ACC rising monotonically with memory size (GEM 0.487 at 200, 0.579 at 1,280, 0.633 at 2,560, 0.654 at 5,120), so the reported results depend materially on a non-trivial memory.

EXTRA:
- GEM's evaluation protocol is a full accuracy matrix R: 'After the model finishes learning about the task t_i, we evaluate its test performance on all T tasks'; and 'For a fine-grained evaluation that accounts for learning speed, one can build a matrix R with more rows than tasks, by evaluating more often [...] Plotting each column of R results into a learning curve.' (Lopez-Paz & Ranzato, Sec. 2). This is a direct template for an interference/restart protocol: re-score every previously taught item after each new write.
- Repeated passes over a task increase forgetting for memory-less learners: 'Multiple training passe[s] exacerbate the catastrophic forgetting problem' — Table 3 (MNIST Rotations) shows 'single' BWT going from -0.08 (1 epoch) to -0.25 (2 epochs) to -0.40 (5 epochs), while 'memory-based methods such as EWC and GEM lead to higher ACC as the number of passes through the data increases'. (Lopez-Paz & Ranzato, Sec. 4.4.1, Table 3). Relevant to a writer that applies delta-rule updates for the same fact more than once.
- GEM frames past-task losses as inequality constraints rather than distillation targets: 'we could keep the predictions at past tasks invariant by means of distillation. However, this would deem positive backward transfer impossible. Instead, we will use the losses (5) as inequality constraints, avoiding their increase but allowing their decrease.' (Lopez-Paz & Ranzato, Sec. 3). A correction protocol should therefore permit positive BWT rather than treat any change to old items as failure.
- Chaudhry et al. define a plasticity counterpart to forgetting, intransigence I_k = a*_k − a_{k,k} where a*_k is the accuracy of a reference model trained on the union of all data up to task k (Eq. 4, Sec. 3), and diagnose capacity with the pair: 'if one observes high negative correlation between forgetting and intransigence, which is usually the case, then, it suggests that either the model capacity is saturated or the method does not effectively utilize the capacity.' (Sec. 3, opening paragraph). Useful for characterizing delta-rule memory capacity/interference: measure both.
- Chaudhry et al. Sec. 2.1 ('Single-head vs Multi-head Evaluations'): 'at test time, in single-head, the task identifier (k) is unknown, whereas in multi-head, it is given'; 'single-head is a more practical setting because knowing the subset of labels to look at a priori [...] requires extra supervisory signals, in the form of task descriptors, at test time that reduce the problem complexity.' They also report that 'storing just a few representative samples [...] from the previous tasks improves intransigence significantly' (Sec. 3, Intransigence paragraph; experiments use 10 samples on MNIST, 25 on CIFAR, Table 1 caption). Queries scored with no access to the teaching context correspond to the single-head/Class-IL regime.
- van de Ven & Tolias: in the Class-IL scenario (task identity must be inferred) 'regularization-based methods such as EWC and SI completely fail' and 'currently only replay-based methods are capable of producing acceptable results' (Sec. 6); Appendix C: 'even storing one example per class was enough for any of the exact replay methods to outperform all regularization-based methods'. Their protocols assume clear task boundaries with multiple passes (2000 iterations/task split MNIST, 5000 permuted MNIST, batch 128; Sec. 4) and they report only 'Average test accuracy (over all tasks)' as mean ± SEM over 20 seeds (Table 4 caption), not BWT/FWT or the max-based forgetting measure.

### [PARTIAL] C2. 'turn left' variant: holding out 'turn left' as the isolated primitive gives much better generalization because 'turn left' shares structure with other commands.
  LOC: Lake & Baroni 2018, 'Experiment 3: Generalizing composition across primitive commands', results paragraph
  PASSAGE: For "turn left", many models generalize very well to composed commands. The best performance is achieved by a GRU network with attention, one layer with 100 hidden units, and dropout of 0.1 (90.3% accuracy). The overall-best model achieved 90.0% accuracy.
  NOTE: The numbers (90.3% / 90.0% vs 1.2% / 0.08%) are verified. The mechanism as phrased in the claim is not quite what the paper says. The paper's explanation is output-side: the ACTION 'LTURN' appears in many other training targets, not that the command 'turn left' shares input structure. Verbatim: 'In the case of "turn left", although models are only exposed to the primitive command during training, they will see the action it denotes (LTURN) many times, as it is used to accomplish many directed actions. For example, a training item is: "walk left and jump left", with ground-truth interpretation: LTURN WALK LTURN JUMP. ... On the other hand, the action denoted by "jump" (JUMP) only occurs with this primitive command in training'. Also relevant: the residual errors were unsystematic: 'all 45 errors made by the model are conjunctions where one of the components is simple "turn left" (22 cases) or "turn left thrice" (23 cases)'. Suggested rewording for the memo: generalization succeeds when the held-out primitive's OUTPUT symbol already occurs inside other trained outputs.
